# Red Star Gale and the Grid of Despair
An amber-hued Martian storm swept in from beyond the horizon like an enraged ancient behemoth, tearing furiously at the radiation-shielding plastic film that encased the base’s hull. The screeching friction mingled with the hissing patter of toxic perchlorate dust against the bulkhead, making the interior of the *Freedom* base feel like the inside of a tin can being battered by a giant hammer.

In the main control room, red alarm lights flashed wildly, casting a flickering, lurid glow over faces pale with terror.

"The main grid load has spiked to 140 percent!"

Schmidt, the German engineer, clutched his hair—his once-meticulously groomed blond locks now a chaotic tangle. His bloodshot eyes were fixed on an old monitor—a piece of Earth-discarded tech patched together with jumper wires and duct tape—as his voice cracked and strained from sheer panic.

"The secondary transformer has blown! The battery bank temperature is plummeting exponentially! If we don't manually cut the load to the south wing within three minutes, the base's thermal control system will fail completely. In less than twenty minutes, the seven of us will be nothing more than the freshest frozen specimens on the Martian surface!"

A deathly silence fell over the control room.

Leo, the young surveyor, huddled in a corner clutching his knees; his lips were drained of color, and he was trembling uncontrollably. Elena, the botanist, bit her lower lip so hard her fingernails dug deep into her palms. Meanwhile, Marcus—formerly a doctor—frantically rummaged through the medical kit for sedatives that had long since run out.

In this drafty, illegal Martian outpost—built by black-market stowaways—the so-called "elite" were as fragile as a crumpled scrap of waste paper when faced with the raw, unbridled power of the cosmos. "Schmidt! Stop acting like a damn radio announcer!"

Mateo, a heavy machinery mechanic, wrenched open the jammed console panel; a spray of sparks made him growl in pain as they seared his skin. "The backup breaker is at the external port outside the airlock—who’s going to throw the switch manually?!"

The storm outside was raging at its peak, with wind speeds exceeding 150 kilometers per hour. That meant anyone stepping even half a foot out of the airlock would be instantly swept away by the gale or suffocate in the toxic, minus-sixty-degree dust storm.

Everyone instinctively took a half-step back. No one wanted to go to their death.

Just as despair began to spread like poisonous ivy through the cramped control cabin, that massive, clumsy hunk of scrap metal—which had been sitting in the corner against the wall—suddenly emitted a heavy, mechanical whir.

*Click... creak...*

It was the harsh, grinding sound of dried-up hydraulic fluid and gears struggling to mesh.

Then, the heavy industrial robot—coated in peeling yellow paint and covered in dents and rust, designated **UNIT 74**—slowly straightened its massive mechanical frame. A faint yet remarkably steady orange-red glow flickered from the heat vent on its chest, marking the location of its neural network core.

Ignoring Mateo’s question, it moved with a heavy, grating mechanical gait, rolling straight toward Schmidt, who looked on in utter breakdown.

Its mechanical feet slammed against the metal floor with a dull *thud-thud* sound, stopping only when it was less than thirty centimeters away from Schmidt.

The air seemed to fill with the phantom scent of cheap tobacco mixed with machine oil. The robot’s optical lens contracted slightly, emitting the voice of a middle-aged man—digitally reproduced, raspy, and laced with heavy static:

"Schmidt, get that sweaty, foul mug of yours out of my face. The CO2 you're exhaling is putting a serious strain on my air purification systems."

Schmidt froze, and the surrounding arguments came to an abrupt halt. Everyone stared in astonishment at this second-hand robot—a machine that was supposed to do nothing but execute rigid, mindless commands.

UNIT 74’s mechanical arm roughly shoved Schmidt aside; then, turning around, it used its heavy metal fingers to precisely pick up a pair of grease-stained insulated pliers and a welding torch.

"Back when I was training a bunch of morons at a machine shop in Nantong, Earth, I saw transformers burn to a crisp more times than I can count. Whining won't fix a gas leak, and it sure as hell won't bring the power grid back to life."

With its core glowing a searing, brilliant light, the unit took heavy, determined strides toward the airlock door—a barrier that could be blown down by the gale-force winds at any moment:

"Sato, bring me the spare fuse. Mateo, get ready to hand me the wrench. And wipe those tears away—as long as this hunk of junk is here today, this rotten red planet isn't taking any of us."

Amidst the heavy hiss of depressurization and the harsh crackle of electric arcs, UNIT 74 plunged headlong into the storm of flying sparks without a second thought.
